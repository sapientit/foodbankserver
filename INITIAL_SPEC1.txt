#Food Bank System
This system is for the management of a food bank.  The food bank runs multiple sessions every week.  People from authorised organisations, or authorised people, can refer people to the food bank.  This referral includes the session they are attending, how the parcel is collected, number of adults and children, reason for referral, name, address, phone number and food preferences.
The administrators can move people between sessions and cancel people.
Each session generates a food pick list - one "parcel" for each person.  The parcel contents depends on the size of the household being supported.
The pick list can then be modified before being printed.  It is printed only after every parcel on it has been reviewed: a parcel still needing a team leader's decision must not reach paper.  Once printed, further changes can be made until the session itself is confirmed.

#Session maintenance
Standard sessions repeat weekly.  They have a default capacity of 25.  It is possible for the administrator to change the time of an individual session, cancel a session or add new ad hoc session.  Fields are date, time, duration, location, capacity and a capacity for deliveries within it.   Recurring sessions or individual session can be created or changed.

A session has a delivery window as well as a start time, because the van does not go out at the moment the hall opens and it cannot arrive at a single minute either.  It is the window the food bank tells a household to expect their delivery in, and it is only that - nothing is scheduled or routed from it, and the driver's round is still made up from the addresses already on the referrals.  A window has a start and an end, and they are set together or not at all - half a window is not a window.  The end has to be later than the start, so that the single moment this exists to get rid of cannot be set again under another name.  A session that does not set a window delivers across the session's own hours, and that is what its households are told.

Before the referral form will accept a request for a delivery it asks the referrer to confirm two things together: that the client meets the food bank's criteria for a delivery, and that the client will be at home for the delivery window.

A session's capacity for deliveries is a number within its overall capacity, not a separate flag.  A session of capacity 25 might allow only 8 of those places to be deliveries, or all 25, or none - the two numbers are independent except that the second can never exceed the first.  A session that takes no deliveries at all is simply one whose delivery capacity is nought; there is nothing else to set to say so.  This is recorded and shown wherever the session is.  A referrer sees words rather than numbers - whether a delivery can still be booked, not how many places are left - because a referrer is being told whether they may promise one, not given a running total.  An administrator or team leader sees the number instead: every session they can see carries how many deliveries have been booked against its delivery capacity, so a member of staff can see which sessions are filling up without opening each one.  Where a session takes no deliveries at all, or its delivery places have all been taken, the form says so in place of the window.  The form now refuses a delivery request once a session's delivery places are gone, and refuses one outright where the session takes no deliveries at all - a hard stop, the same as a session that has reached its overall capacity, and for the same reason: a promise the food bank cannot keep should not be one a referrer is left to make and an administrator left to catch afterwards.  A collection is never affected by a session's delivery capacity, however full it is; only a delivery is turned away.

A session cannot be cancelled while anybody still holds a place on it.  The households have to be dealt with first - moved to another session, or cancelled - and only then can the session itself go.  Cancelling a session out from under the people booked onto it would leave families expecting food that nobody has arranged, and no record that anyone ever decided what to do about them.  Making the administrator move them first means somebody has looked at every household and chosen, rather than a single click quietly stranding twenty-five of them.  It follows that a cancelled session never has referrals attached to it.

An administrator can also delete a weekly session record outright - set up in error, or no longer wanted - and this is a different act from cancelling one of its sessions.  Deleting the weekly record does not delete the sessions it has already put on the calendar: those stay exactly as they are, still editable, still cancellable, still carrying whatever referrals are on them, just no longer tied to any weekly pattern.  It is only the pattern itself that goes, and only future sessions stop being generated from it - nothing already scheduled is taken away.

The sessions in the calendar are real occurrences that have been generated ahead of time, not dates worked out on the fly whenever somebody looks.  That is what makes each one editable in its own right.  Once an administrator has re-timed or cancelled a particular week, that change stands: generating the calendar again never writes over an occurrence that already exists, so an amendment made today is still there next week and nobody has to make it twice.

Capacity counts households, not people.  A session of capacity 25 takes 25 referrals however large the households are, so it may well feed a hundred people.  A referral occupies one place whether it is a single person or a family of six, because it is one parcel to pick and one person coming to collect it.  A cancelled referral gives its place back, and so does a rejected one.

A referral waiting to be reviewed holds its place just like any other.  Nobody has looked at it yet, but the household may well be coming, and the food bank would rather see a session fill up with people it might have to feed than tell a team leader on the morning of a session that five more have just been let in and there is no parcel for them.  So the public list stops offering a session once the referrals it has - reviewed or not - reach its capacity.

A list of available sessions for the next 2 weeks can be made without signon. A session is not available if it is at or over capacity.

Referrals for a session close at four in the afternoon the day before it runs, and the public list stops offering it once four has gone by.  At five to four on Monday next day's Tuesday session is still on the list, and at four o'clock exactly it still is - four is the time a referrer is asked to be in by, so somebody arriving on the hour has made it.  At one minute past it is gone, and the soonest session offered is Wednesday's.  A session is never offered on its own day.  The food bank picks for a session the afternoon before, so a referral arriving that evening is a household nobody has packed food for.

The cutoff also applies to the referral submit as well as to the list.  A referral submitted against a session whose cutoff has passed is refused outright - a hard stop, the same as a session that has reached capacity - because the food bank has already picked for the session the afternoon before, and a referral arriving after that is for a household nobody has packed food for.  A referrer whose session closes while they are still filling in the form sees the same refusal, and starts again with the next session the list offers.

After signon there is a list of sessions, and how far ahead it reaches depends on who is looking.  An administrator sees the whole six weeks that have been generated, because rearranging the calendar that far ahead is their job and they cannot move a session they cannot see.  A team leader sees six days, counted from today and including it, so they have the week of shifts in front of them and nothing else.  A team leader runs the session that is coming; the calendar six weeks out is a planning tool and it belongs to the administrator.  The six days are a limit on looking forward and not on looking back - the session just gone is still on a team leader's list.

The six days shape the list and nothing else.  A team leader who already has a particular session in hand can still open it and work on its picking list however far out it is, and the food bank does not want that prevented: a team leader getting a fortnight's picking ready in advance is doing their job, not getting round a rule.  In practice a team leader will never be looking more than six days ahead, so this is a matter of showing them the right week rather than of keeping them out of the wrong one, and it is not something the system needs to police.

The two week public list is therefore longer than the team leader's six days, which is deliberate rather than an oversight.  They answer different questions: a referrer needs to know which sessions they can still book somebody onto, and needs enough notice to arrange it, while a team leader needs the shifts they are about to work.

#Authorised referrers
Referrers can be authorised either by full email address or a star and domain name.(*@guildford.gov.uk).  There is no check that they are who they say they are - only that the email address they enter is valid.

An address that is not on the list does not stop the referral being made.  The food bank would rather look at a referral it was not expecting than turn away a household that needs feeding on the strength of a list that is always slightly out of date - a new social worker, a charity nobody has added yet, somebody using their home address because they are working from home.  The referral is taken and waits for an administrator, who accepts it or rejects it.  Until then it is still picked, so the people running the session have its parcel, referral and name with every household they may text.

An administrator looking at a referral that is waiting because the address was not recognised has two ways of accepting it.  They can accept that one referral and leave the list alone, or they can accept it and put the referrer on the authorised list at the same time, so that the next referral from that person is not held up.  The second of those adds the referrer's own email address and only that address - never the whole domain, because one person the food bank has decided to trust is not the same as everybody who works where they work.  The administrator types the organisation name themselves when they do it, and confirms it, rather than the food bank taking whatever the referrer typed on the form: the authorised list is what organisations are reported on, and it fills up with three spellings of the same council otherwise.

The organisation names on the list are also offered to the referrer as a dropdown when they fill the form in, with a free text box beside it for an organisation that is not there.  Typing into that box is the ordinary way a referral comes to be waiting for review.

#referral
The referral is currently a Google Form because it does change periodically.  Like Google forms, the questions have a column key and validation rules.  There will be some columns that are fixed, but many of the columns will have to be generic names in the database.

The form is held as a configuration file belonging to the referral application, not in the server and not in the database.  A change to the questions is made in that configuration, seen and tried in the test system first, and goes live when the new version of the application does.  That is the publishing mechanism, so there is no draft and publish step to build and no form maintenance screen.

The server does not hold the questions and does not check the answers against them.  It stores the dynamic answers exactly as they are given and hands them back the same way, so a referral captured under an older version of the form stays readable - each answer arrives with the key it was asked under.  The fixed columns below are the only part of a referral the server has an opinion about.

The fixed columns are: date of referral, session id, name of referrer, email id of referrer, organisation of referrer, phone number of referrer, first name of referee, surname of referee, date of birth of referee, address of referee, postcode of referee, phone number of referee, the number of adults, the number of children, how the parcel is collected, whether the household needs help with gas or electricity, and reason for referral.  All other columns are dynamic.

Household composition is one dynamic JSON answer, with the fixed key `Household Components`, presented to the referrer as a grid.  Its rows are ages 0-4, 5-11, 12-17, 18 to State Pension age, and State Pension age or over; its columns are Female, Male, Non-Binary and Prefer not to say.  Empty cells are shown blank and omitted from the JSON; every stored cell is a whole number from one to thirty.  It is collected for reporting, not as a second set of operational totals.

The client derives the fixed counts from the grid and submits them with the referral.  They are sizing figures for the household grid rather than a description of who lives there, and which ages fall into which is the client's business alone: the server is never told what an adult is and must never work it out from the composition answer.  It validates the two numbers as numbers, uses only them for the household grid and automatic preference rules, and stores the composition answer without interpreting its age or gender detail.  That is what lets the charity change where the line falls without a change to the server or a migration of what it holds.  A referral must have at least one adult, meaning only that the count the client sends is at least one.

The referee's name is held as a first name and a surname rather than one line.  The surname is what a list is sorted by and what a volunteer reads when they are matching a bag to the person standing in front of them, and a name that has been run together cannot be taken apart again afterwards.

The referee's date of birth is held rather than their age, because it is used to identify the individual for future referrals or when the client phones the food bank..

The organisation of the referrer is what the referrer said it was.  A referrer the food bank does not recognise has no entry to take it from, which is the whole reason the form asks.

The referrer's own phone number is required on every referral, not only a `referrer_collect` one - the food bank may need to ring the person who sent a household, whatever the parcel's own collection method turns out to be.

Whether the household needs help with gas or electricity is a fixed column because the food bank reports on it.  The questions that follow from it are ordinary questions on the form and are not reported on in the same way.  

How a parcel is collected is a fixed column, `collection method`, and it is the only source of truth for whether a parcel is a delivery - there is no separate delivery flag for a referrer or an administrator to disagree with it.  It holds one of three values.  `collection` is the referee coming to the session themselves.  `delivery` goes to the referee's own address - there is no separate delivery address, and none is wanted, the one address held on the referral is both where the referee lives and where a delivery is taken.  `referrer_collect` is the referrer collecting the parcel on the referee's behalf, because it is sometimes the referrer rather than the client who can get to the session.  Only `delivery` counts as a delivery; `referrer_collect` is a collection in every other respect - a session's delivery capacity does not see it, the parcel is ready at the session the same as any other collection, and nobody is driving anywhere for it.  The driver's round is made up from the addresses already on the referrals whose collection method is `delivery`.

A referral captured before collection method existed only ever recorded true-or-false for a delivery, so it is read across as `delivery` where that was true and `collection` where it was not.  Nothing already held can say whether a referrer was collecting, so none of them are read across as `referrer_collect` - that is not a guess the food bank's own records can settle after the fact.

There is a maximum capacity on each session, and a further capacity for deliveries within it, so referrals cannot be made to a full session and a delivery cannot be made once a session's delivery places are gone.  Nor can a referral be made once a session's cutoff, described under "Session maintenance", has passed.  All three are refused outright rather than left for an administrator to catch.

Households are reminded of their session by text message, and a referral records that a reminder has been sent to it.  Reminders and the replies to them are described under "SMS reminders and replies" below.

Note that the referral is done without authentication (other than checking the entered email address).  Once it is submitted it cannot be changed by the referrer.  They are shown a confirmation of what they sent, with the fixed answers on it so they can see it is right, and that is the end of their part in it.  A referrer who then needs a change rings the food bank and an administrator makes it.

The reason for referral is chosen from a list the administrators maintain rather than typed in.  It is there to be reported on - how many households came for which reason - and free text cannot be counted.  It also means the reason can be kept after the personal details have been removed, because a reason from a fixed list says nothing about who the person was.

#Forgetting a referral
Fifteen months after the referral was made, the food bank deletes it outright.  Nothing about it survives: not the household's own details, not the counts derived from them, not the referrer who sent it, not the comment an administrator wrote explaining why it was accepted or rejected.  The referral is gone, in the same way it would be if it had never been made - not held in a reduced or anonymised form.

Its parcel goes with it, pick-list information and all.  A parcel is the record of what one household was given at one session, and once the referral it belonged to is gone there is no household left for that record to be about - keeping the parcel on its own would be a shelf of allergy notes and preferences with nobody's name left to attach them to.  The session's own figures - what was picked, what stock moved, who attended - are not the parcel; they stand regardless of whether any one household's parcel still exists.

Fifteen months is what the charity is prepared to defend holding somebody's name and address for.  It is also the shortest period that leaves the repeat-referral count on the review screen telling the truth, since that count looks back fifteen months and matches on the very details deletion removes.  The two were settled together and moving either without the other quietly breaks it: shortening this makes the review screen report households as new when they are not.

Deleting rather than anonymising costs this system its own reporting beyond fifteen months, but not the food bank's: every signed-off session is already sent to the extract spreadsheet (see "Sending referrals to the spreadsheet" below) with the household's name, address, email and phone number left off but its postcode, date of birth, reason for referral, answers and household composition kept, and that copy is never deleted or rewritten.  A shape like "we fed 340 households, 890 people, 22% for benefit delay" for longer than fifteen months, or a year-on-year comparison, is answered from the spreadsheet rather than from this system - which is a large part of why the spreadsheet exists rather than being optional.  What this system cannot do past fifteen months is name a household or act on its referral; it was never going to be the place the charity did its longer-term reporting from.

#Reviewing a referral
A referral from an address that is not on the authorised list is held for an administrator, who either accepts it or rejects it.  An accepted referral is from then on an ordinary referral.  A rejected one is finished with: it releases its place on the session and it never reaches a picking list, a printed sheet or the household grid, because a parcel picked for somebody who was turned away is stock and volunteer time spent on nobody.  A rejected referral cannot then be cancelled either.  Being rejected and being cancelled are different things that happened, and the comment explaining the rejection is the only record of why the food bank said no - calling it a cancellation afterwards would leave that comment on a referral that no longer says anybody ever reviewed it.

Each review carries a single line of comment - why it was let through, or why it was not.  It is not a history: reviewing the same referral again replaces what was there.  The comment is not timestamped.  The referral itself is timestamped, at the moment the referrer submitted it, and that is the time the food bank cares about.

The comment is seen by administrators only.  It can name a referrer or set down a suspicion about one, and that is not something to put in front of the volunteer running the session.

Team leaders do see referrals that are waiting to be reviewed, marked as not yet confirmed.  A team leader is the person standing in the hall when a household arrives, and being unable to answer "are you expecting us?" helps nobody.  They do not see rejected referrals at all - a rejected referral is not their concern, and the only thing that would explain it is the comment they are not shown.

From a session they are running, a team leader can open a referral-details list for that session and print it when needed.  It carries the client name, address, postcode and phone number, the referrer's name, organisation and phone number, and the household's pick number.  The organisation is there because the name on its own does not always say who has rung: the person running the session is as likely to need to know that a household was sent by the council or by a school as to need the name of the individual who sent them, and a team leader who is going to ring the referrer back should be able to see where they are ringing.  The pick number is there so that a team leader on the phone to a late household can give the picker its number over the same call, rather than having to look the household up a second time on the picking sheets.  It does not otherwise carry the reason for referral, date of birth, answers, review comment, the referrer's email address, the administrators' note or parcel contents.  A household with no pick number yet - because no pick list has been generated for the session, or because the household was referred after one was - simply shows none; unlike the listener sheet, this list is not refused over it, since it exists so the team can ring a household, not to match it against the picking sheets.  The list is limited to the households on that session that the team lead is otherwise allowed to see; rejected and cancelled referrals are not slipped back into view by this route.

Every referral is meant to be read by an administrator, not only the ones held because the address was not recognised, and a referral says whether that has happened yet.  So a referral moves on: it is waiting for review if the address was not recognised, then it is accepted, and once an administrator has read it through it is reviewed.  A referral from a recognised address starts as accepted and is still to be read.  The point of it is the list of referrals nobody has looked at yet, so that an administrator can work through them and see the pile go down.

A reviewed referral is an ordinary referral in every other way.  It holds its place on the session exactly as an accepted one does, it is picked, and it appears on the picking list and the listener sheet - the only thing being reviewed says is that somebody has read it.  Cancelling a referral takes it out whether it had been reviewed or not, and the food bank does not need to know afterwards whether a cancelled referral had been read.

A food bank parcel is emergency support and not a way of living.  The intention is that a household is referred once, while their benefits are being sorted out or whatever else has gone wrong is put right.  A household coming back again and again is not a fraud to be caught but a sign that the emergency was never resolved, and the administrator reviewing the referral is the person who should see that and decide what to do about it.

So the review screen shows, for the household being reviewed, how many times they have already been referred in the last fifteen months, and the date of the session the most recent of those was for.  A button beside it lists those referrals, most recent first, each one shown in full: the name, address, phone number, postcode and date of birth, along with the date of that referral's session and what became of it.

The duplicate-referrals section starts by matching on all three identifiers.  It has an `Exclude postcode matches` checkbox, initially unticked.  When an administrator ticks it, both the count and the list are recalculated using only date of birth and phone number.  A shared postcode in a hostel or refuge is useful to see by default, but an administrator must be able to remove it from the calculation without changing the referral itself.

Administrators can also search referrals directly by postcode, phone number and/or date of birth, with an optional start of surname to narrow what those find.  Supplying more than one identifier broadens the search: a referral matching any supplied identifier is shown.  A result gives the administrator enough to recognise the household and answer the person on the phone, and no more; #Searching for a referral sets out exactly what, and why each of the things left out is left out.  It is administrator-only because the search identifiers and results are personal information.

What counts is every referral the food bank has not turned away.  A rejected or cancelled referral does not count; everything else does, including a household who was booked in and did not turn up, and one whose session has not come round yet.  A household referred to two sessions in the same week is exactly what this screen exists to catch, and were the count to be of parcels actually handed over, that second referral would stay invisible until after it had been picked, packed and given out - which is the moment it is too late to do anything about it.  So the number says how many times this household has been sent to the food bank rather than how much food they have been given, and each referral on the list says which of the three it was - given their parcel, did not turn up, or still to come - so that the administrator reading it can tell them apart.

The most recent of those can be a date still to come, and the screen shows it as it stands: a household booked in for next Tuesday is reported as next Tuesday.  The referral being reviewed is never one of its own matches.

The list stops at fifty, the fifty most recent.  Past that nobody is going to read on, so listing more would not tell an administrator anything they were going to use - and a screen holding several hundred other households' names, addresses and phone numbers, assembled because somebody opened one unrelated referral, is not something the food bank wants to have built.  The count is not capped and still says how many there really are, so a list of fifty against a count of three hundred says plainly that fifty is not all of them.  It is a postcode shared by a hostel or a refuge that produces numbers like that, and the honest answer to the administrator in that case is the count and a sample of it, not three hundred names.

The date shown for each referral is the date of the session it is on, because that is the day the household was fed or is to be fed, and that is what the administrator is judging.  The fifteen months, though, are counted back from when the referral was made, which is the same clock the food bank forgets a household by - so the count never reaches back past details that have already gone.

The matching is approximate and the system does not draw a conclusion from it.  Nothing is blocked, refused or flagged automatically: the administrator is shown what was found and makes the call.  A number on a screen is the right amount of help here, and a rule that refused a household on the strength of a shared postcode would be the wrong kind.

Three things are matched on: date of birth, postcode, and phone number.  Any one of them matching is enough.  Names are deliberately not matched on, because different people from the same household get referred at different times and the same name is typed differently by different referrers, so a name is the least reliable of the things the food bank holds.

Each match says which of the three it matched on, because they are not equally convincing.  A match on all three is almost certainly the same household; a match on postcode alone may be nothing more than two families in the same block of flats, a hostel or a refuge - which is exactly the housing the households the food bank serves are most likely to be in.  An administrator who is told only "three previous matches" cannot tell those apart, and would learn to ignore the number.

Matching needs the three fields in a settled form - a postcode typed with no space and one typed in lower case are the same postcode, and a phone number is the same number however it was written.  That standardising is for matching only, and the postcode, phone number and date of birth are stored as they were written; putting a value into a settled form never rewrites the value itself.

An administrator can correct any of those three, so the settled form has to be worked out again whenever one of them is corrected.  A household matched on an address that has since been fixed is matched on nothing; and the correction is often the very thing that makes the match, because a postcode typed wrongly the first time is exactly how the same household comes to look like two.

The referral form puts the postcode into that settled form as it is typed, and the food bank does the same again when the referral arrives.  Doing it twice is deliberate: the form is the better place to do it, because it can show the referrer what has been understood while they can still correct it, but a referral can reach the food bank by other means and the matching cannot depend on every one of them having tidied up first.  For the two to agree, the rule has to be written down where both can read it rather than settled once at each end.

Putting a value into a settled form is not the same as insisting on one.  A postcode the rule cannot make sense of does not stop the referral: it is taken, stored as typed, and simply not matched on.  The food bank would rather look at a referral with an odd postcode on it than turn away a household because of the way an address was written down.

A field that is missing is not a match either.  The phone number is optional, and two households that both have no phone number have nothing in common - that is an absence, not a shared detail.  The same holds for a postcode or a phone number the rule could make nothing of.  Only a value the food bank actually holds and understands can match another.

The fifteen months cannot reach further back than the food bank keeps personal details for.  Once a referral's personal details have been forgotten there is nothing left to match on, and the household is not found.  That is accepted rather than worked around: the food bank is not going to hold names and addresses for longer than it otherwise would in order to make this count go back further.

#Searching for a referral
Administrators have a screen of their own for finding a referral when somebody rings up.  It searches on date of birth, postcode and phone number - any one of them, or more than one - and it is administrators only.  Date of birth is the normal way to find somebody; postcode and phone number are alternatives.  A search with none of the three filled in is refused rather than answered, because a search that matched on nothing would return every household the food bank has ever fed.  An optional start of surname is a narrowing filter, not a search of its own: it is applied alongside at least one of the three identifiers, to separate the unlikely clients who share a date of birth.

The three are the same three the duplicate count matches on, put into the same settled form by the same rule.  There is no second definition of what makes two postcodes the same one, and a value the rule cannot make sense of is simply not searched on, exactly as it is not matched on.

More than one identifier widens the search rather than narrowing it.  A referral matching any one of the values given is found.  Somebody ringing up gives the details they have and often gets one of them slightly wrong - a transposed digit, last year's address - and a search that insisted on all of them agreeing would fail at the moment it was most needed.  A supplied start of surname narrows that widened set, case-insensitively, by the beginning of the stored surname.  It does not change how the three identifiers are matched.

A result gives the administrator the session date, what became of the referral, the client's first name and surname, postcode and phone number, the main cause of referral, the referrer's name and the organisation they referred from, the referral's answers, and the administrators' own note about the household.  The note is on this screen and on no other list of households, for the reason given under "Referral maintenance"; a referral with no note on it simply shows none.  The organisation is what an administrator recognises a referral by on this screen: the individual who sent it changes with who was on shift that week, while the organisation is what the household names on the phone.  Both are given, because the name is what an administrator needs once they have to ring the referrer back.  A row may use two lines rather than forcing this information into an unreadably wide table.  The main cause is resolved against the maintained reasons lookup.  The secondary cause of referral and the additional crisis information are among the answers rather than fields of their own: the answers belong to the referral application, which is the only part of the system that knows what any of them mean, so they are handed over whole and it takes out the ones the screen shows.  The result does not give the date of birth, the address, the comment an administrator wrote when reviewing the referral - which is a different thing from the note about the household, and stays behind the opened referral - the referrer's email or the referrer's phone number.

The search reaches every referral the food bank still holds details for, whatever became of it - cancelled and rejected ones included, because "we turned that one away in March" is exactly what the person on the phone is ringing about.  It does not reach a referral whose details have been forgotten: there is nothing left to search on, and that is the same accepted limit the duplicate count has.

The number found is reported even when the list is not all of it.  At most fifty are listed, the most recent session first, and the count says how many there really are - so a list of fifty against a count of two hundred says plainly that fifty is not all of them, which is the honest answer for a postcode that belongs to a hostel.

#Referral maintenance.
Administrators can cancel referrals or move them to another session (even if that exceeds capacity with a client generated warning).  A referral that has been cancelled or rejected cannot be amended, and cannot be moved either - there is nothing that brings a referral back, so moving a finished one only shifts a dead record from one session to another.

A referral says what became of the household as well as what became of the referral.  The two are different questions and are answered separately.  What became of the referral is whether it is waiting to be reviewed, accepted, read, rejected or cancelled; what became of the household is what happened on the day - they collected their parcel, they did not turn up, or they are still to come.  A household is still to come whether their parcel has been picked and is waiting for somebody to mark it or no picking list has been made for their session yet, because both mean the same thing to somebody reading the screen: nothing has happened yet.  A cancelled referral reads as still to come as well, for the same reason - nothing happened on the day, and the cancellation is recorded where cancellations are recorded.  The two answers must never contradict each other.  They are the same three answers the review screen already gives for each of a household's earlier referrals, and they are deliberately the same words: what happened to a household on a day is one question, not two.  Nobody is shielded from it - a team leader running the session is already told who turned up and who did not.

Moving a household to another session after the picking list has already been made does undo the parcel, and this is the opposite of what cancelling does.  The parcel picked on the session the household is leaving is deleted outright - the parcel and its contents both.  Nothing was handed over and no stock came off the shelves, so there is nothing there to account for: the picking list on the old session is simply wrong about who it is for once the household is not on it.  Left in place it would be worse than untidy, because it would sit on that morning's list as a household still to come, be picked and packed a second time, and be waiting for somebody to say whether they turned up.  The household is picked for again on the session they have moved to, in the ordinary way, and takes a picking number there.

The difference from cancelling is deliberate.  A cancelled household was one of that session's households and the food bank really did plan a parcel for them that morning, so the parcel stays and says so - even though nothing in it ever came off a shelf, because stock only moves when a household is marked as having attended, and a cancelled household never is.  A moved household is not that session's business at all any more, and there is nothing to explain.

A household can only be moved while its parcel is still waiting for an outcome.  Once it has been marked as having turned up or as not having turned up, the referral has finished and cannot be moved - the session it is on is the session it was fed on, or was not, and saying otherwise would record it as due food on a day it has already been dealt with.  This holds even though the session as a whole is still open, because a session stays open until every household on it has an outcome, and one household being finished with does not wait for the rest.  If somebody who was marked as not turning up is to be given another chance, that is a new referral rather than a move of this one, and the way to make it is to copy the referral onto another session as described below.

Cancelling stops at exactly the same point, and for the same reason.  A referral can be cancelled only while it is still waiting for an outcome.  Once a household has been marked as having collected, as having been delivered to, or as not having turned up, cancelling is refused and the administrator is told why rather than being left to think it worked.  Food that has come off the shelves cannot be un-given, and a household who did not turn up did not turn up; in both cases what happened on the day is the record, and cancelling afterwards would leave the parcel's own account of the morning saying one thing and the referral saying another.  If what the administrator meant was that the outcome itself was recorded by mistake, the thing to do is take the outcome back, which is a different button and undoes the stock with it.  If what they meant was that a household who did not turn up should be given another chance, the no show stays where it is and the household gets a copy of their referral on a later session, described below.

A referral whose details have been forgotten no longer exists to act on.  Fifteen months on there is no referral left to correct, decide on, read through, move to another session or copy - it has been deleted, not merely stripped of its details.

Cancelling a household after the picking list has already been made does not undo the parcel.  The parcel stays exactly as it was picked - the same items, the same quantities, the same note and the same picking number - because it is the record of what the food bank had planned to give that household, and a session that has to be accounted for afterwards cannot have that quietly deleted from under it.  None of that is stock moving: a parcel is a plan on a screen, not a bag off a shelf, and stock only moves when a household is marked as having attended.  A cancelled parcel is never marked attended, so its items stay exactly where they were, still on the shelf and free to go into somebody else's parcel.  What changes is that the parcel itself says the household has cancelled.  From that moment it is no longer one of the households the session is being run for: it is not waiting for anybody to review it, it is not waiting to be marked as having turned up or not, it holds no place on the session, it is not printed, and it is not texted.  It stays on the picking list screen, saying plainly that the household cancelled, so that a team leader already holding a printed sheet can see why that number is not coming rather than spending the morning wondering.  A household who has cancelled cannot then be marked as having received their parcel; if somebody turns up after all, that is a new referral and not an undoing of this one, and the way to make it is to copy the cancelled referral onto another session as described below.

An administrator can correct a referral: the referee's name, date of birth, address, postcode and phone number, the household numbers, the delivery and fuel questions, the reason for referral, and the answers given on the form.  A referrer who mistypes an address, or a household that moves between being referred and being fed, has to be correctable - a delivery goes to the address on the referral, so a wrong one there is a parcel left on the wrong doorstep.  Only what is actually changed needs sending; a correction to one field is not a restatement of the referral.

The referrer's own details are not amendable.  Their email address above all, because it is what the decision to accept the referral was made on, and a referral whose address had since been changed would no longer explain its own status.  Their name, organisation and phone number stay with it for the same reason in a weaker form: who sent a referral is a matter of record rather than a field to tidy up.

A correction overwrites, and what the referrer originally sent is not kept.  The food bank does not want a second copy of every referral held against the day somebody asks what an address used to say - that copy would be personal data of its own, outliving the referral it came from and needing forgetting in its own right.

Nor is a record kept of who changed what and when.  The food bank has not asked for an audit trail and does not want one: it is a charity feeding families, not an organisation that needs to reconstruct who edited a postcode eighteen months ago, and a log of every change made to records about vulnerable households is another store of sensitive information to look after, explain and eventually delete.  The people doing this work are trusted volunteers and staff, and the system should treat them that way.  Where it matters who did something - who counted the stock, who marked a household as attended, who reviewed a referral - the record already sits on the thing itself, and that is enough.  Where a stock take was counted by a volunteer on a code rather than by someone signed in (see #Login), what sits on it is the team leader who gave the code out, because handing the counting over is the decision that had a name on it.

The "other information" answer on the form is a free note from the referrer or an administrator to whoever is running the session - the team leader working the picking list, and the person reading the listener sheet.  Almost everything about a referral already has a field of its own, and an administrator can correct any of them outright, as above; "other information" is not where a correction has to go.  Where it earns its place is what a corrected field cannot say on its own - why an address changed, an allergy or access need worth flagging, anything relevant to the day that is not itself one of the facts a field holds.  A corrected address reaches the driver; a note about why it changed reaches the person handing the bag over.  They do different jobs and the food bank wants both.

Which answer is the "other information" one is a matter for the form, so the server takes the answers as a set and does not police which of them changed.

Administrators also keep a note of their own on a referral, separate from the answers the referrer gave.  It is one piece of free text, a couple of paragraphs at most, and it is where the office writes down what it has come to know about the household - what was said when they rang, what the volunteer handing the bag over does not need to be told.  It is written and read by administrators only.  A team leader does not see it, and it is not on the picking list, the listener sheet, the referral-details list, the repeat-referral list, the fuel help list, a text message or the spreadsheet the referrals are sent to.  Nor is it on the list of referrals: on a list an administrator is scanning past, it belongs to the referral they have open rather than to the rows.

The one exception is the search screen described under "Searching for a referral", which does show it on every row, and that is deliberate.  That screen exists for an administrator who is on the phone to a household, and what the office learned the last time it rang them is wanted at the same moment the causes are - having to open the referral to read it is having to open it in the middle of the call.  The screen is administrators only in the first place, so nobody sees a row there who would not have been allowed to open every referral on it.  That is the whole of the exception: the rest of the list above is unchanged, and the note stays off all of it.

The note is not one of the form's answers and is not held with them.  The answers belong to the referral form, and saving a page of that form replaces the whole set of them with what was on it; a note the office wrote must not be carried off by a form it was never part of.  Writing to the note replaces what was there, and it can be emptied again, after which the food bank holds no note on that referral.

#Copying a referral
A household whose referral came to nothing sometimes rings the food bank and is given another chance.  They did not turn up, or their referral was cancelled, or it was rejected and the charity has since thought better of it.  What happens then is that the referral is copied onto a later session.  The original is the record of what actually happened and is never reopened - a no show stays a no show, on the day it happened - and the copy is an ordinary new referral that starts again from there.

Copying is offered only where the original can no longer come to anything: it was cancelled, or it was rejected, or the household was marked as not having turned up.  A referral that is still on its way to being fed is moved rather than copied, and the two are never offered as alternatives for the same referral, because a choice between relocating a household and duplicating them is a choice an administrator should never have to get right.  A household who has already collected is not copied either: they were fed, and feeding them again is a new referral made in the ordinary way rather than a second go at an old one.

The copy carries everything the food bank knows about the household and about who sent them - the referee's name, date of birth, address, postcode and phone number, the adult and child numbers, whether it is a delivery, whether they need help with fuel, the reason for referral, and every answer given on the form, together with the referrer's name, organisation, email address and phone number.  It carries nothing that belongs to what happened to the original: not its state, not its session, not the comment explaining a review, not the administrators' note about the household, and not its parcel.

The reason for referral comes across even if the charity has since stopped offering it.  The copy records the same crisis the original did, and a household is not made to wait because a list was tidied up in the meantime.

The copy is accepted and read the moment it is made, and carries no review comment.  The administrator making it has just decided this household should come, so there is nothing left for a later administrator to accept and nothing waiting to be read through, and no review has taken place for a comment to explain.  This does mean that copying a rejected referral lets the household in after all, which is the point of it.  The rejection and the comment explaining it stay on the original, where they happened, and do not follow the copy - the charity has changed its mind about this household, not about what it decided last time.

The copy is timestamped at the moment it was made, not at the moment the original was submitted.  It is a new referral and the food bank wants it read as one - on the search screen, which sorts and reads by that date, and in the fifteen months the food bank holds a household's details for, which start again with the copy rather than running out part way through it.

The administrators' note on the copy says which referral it was copied from and the date that one was made, and says nothing else.  It replaces rather than adds to any note the original carried: a copy is a fresh start on what the office knows about the household as much as on anything else, and an administrator who wants what was written before reads the referral it was written on.

Choosing the destination session works exactly as moving does.  A full one is warned about rather than refused, and the administrator confirms it the same way they confirm a move.  This is a different question from why the original is eligible to be copied: a cancelled or rejected original, or one whose household did not turn up, is exactly what this feature is for, above.  But the session it is landing on is a session in its own right, and if that session has itself been cancelled, or has already been confirmed, it cannot take a copy at all - the same as it could not take a move.

Copying makes no parcel.  The copy holds its place on its new session and is picked for in the ordinary way, when the picking list for that session is made or opened again.

Copying the same referral more than once is not guarded against, including onto the same session twice.  An administrator who wants two chances for one household may have them, and one who does it by mistake sees the duplicate sitting on the session's own list rather than anything silent - the same trade the food bank already accepts on a referral submitted twice by the public.

A referral whose details have been forgotten cannot be copied.  There is nothing left to copy.

#Login
Signing on is by Google identity.  Everyone who uses the system has an account on the charity's own Google domain, which the charity controls, and a Google identity from outside that domain is refused even when the address on it matches an account the food bank has created.  That restriction is what makes the Google account the thing being trusted rather than an address typed into a box: somebody who opens a Gmail account in a volunteer's name gets nowhere with it.

Alongside it there is a dummy signon page with no validation, which accepts any address that already has an account.  It exists because the charity's Google domain was not available when the system was built and the rest of the system could not wait for it.  The two sit side by side and a deployment is configured to use one or the other.  The dummy signon is not a fallback that a failed Google signon drops through to - a signon that quietly stops checking anything when the checking breaks is worse than one that fails and says so.  Either way, signing on produces an expiring security token with refresh tokens as described below, and everything after the moment of signing on is the same whichever was used.

The dummy signon is for development only, and the system refuses to start in production while it is configured, so the charity cannot go live on it by accident.  It follows that going live depends on the charity's Google domain being available, and no amount of the rest of the system being finished changes that.

There is one way onto the system that is not a signon at all: the volunteer code a team leader hands out for a stock take, described under #Stock maintenance.  It is a single generated code and nothing else - no account, no address, no Google identity - and a volunteer counting the shelves types that code and starts counting.  It reaches the stock take and reaches nothing else whatever.  It lasts 14 days from the moment it was made and then it stops working; there is nothing to cancel it sooner, and nothing about it is kept once it has lapsed.  Making another code does not cancel or replace an earlier one: each remains usable until its own expiry time.  It creates no account and names nobody: what is done with it is recorded against the team leader who gave it out.

A signon lasts 8 hours and then the person signs in again.  The 8 hours are counted from the moment they signed in and carrying on working does not push them back, so a signon that started at eight in the morning has ended by four in the afternoon whatever has happened in between.  Eight hours covers a session in the warehouse without a team leader being thrown out halfway through marking attendance, and it means a tablet left on a shared bench is not still signed in the next morning.  Within those 8 hours the client renews its security token quietly in the background, so nobody is asked to sign in again while they are working.

The timeout should not be aggressive in any other way.  In particular, a security token that has already been renewed is refused if it is presented again - it is spent, and one renewal is all it is good for - but the person holding the live signon carries on working.  A repeated request is far more often a client that retried than a stolen token, and signing somebody out of every device they use on that evidence disrupts the charity more than the thing it is guarding against.

Only people the food bank has already given an account to can sign in.  Signing in never creates an account: an administrator adds the person first, and an address with no account is refused however convincingly they proved who they are.  There is no self-service signup.  Accounts are never deleted, because the stock records and the attendance records name the people who made them; an account that is no longer needed is made inactive instead, and an inactive account cannot sign in.

A new database starts with a single administrator account already in it, because otherwise there is nobody who could create the first one and nobody who could create the person who creates everyone else.  That account is pete@x.com for the time being.  It is a stand-in rather than the charity's own address, and it is replaced when signon moves to Google.

#Roles
There are three roles, administrator, team leader and fuel administrator.

Team leaders run the sessions.  That means the picking list, printing it, and marking who attended.  It also means the stock itself - doing the weekly stock take - because the team leader is the person standing in the warehouse.  They also see why each household was referred: the listener sheet is a team leader job, not an administrator one, and it needs the reason for referral on it.  The reason is chosen from a fixed list rather than typed, so it says what kind of crisis brought the household in without saying anything about the household itself.

Administrators do everything a team leader does - without exception, so there is never a screen or an action open to a team leader that is closed to an administrator - and additionally maintain the list of stock items, the authorised referrers, the reasons for referral, the model parcels and household grid, the user accounts, and amendments to sessions and referrals.  The referral form itself is not on that list: it is application configuration rather than data, so it changes with a release rather than on a maintenance screen.

The stock item list is the one part of stock that is administrator only.  Adding an item or changing its shelf number changes every picking list and every stock take that follows, so it is a maintenance decision rather than a warehouse one.

A fuel administrator does one job: the fuel help list, described below.  The role exists because that job is done by somebody who is not otherwise part of running the food bank, and it carries names, addresses and phone numbers, so it is deliberately not a variety of administrator.  A fuel administrator sees the fuel help list and nothing else at all - no sessions, no referrals, no listener sheet, no stock, no user accounts.  The role is a boundary rather than a job title: it is what stops somebody brought in to help with fuel bills from being able to read why every household in the borough was referred.  Administrators can produce the fuel help list as well, so that the charity is not locked out of its own report when the person who normally runs it is away or their account has been made inactive.  Team leaders cannot: it is not something that happens on the day.

#Stock maintenance
There are about 120 stock items, with a maintenance facility to amend them.
Stock is maintained in a certain place in the warehouse. So each stock item has a "shelf number" .  A stock item is a name, a description, a category, a shelf number and a low stock threshold.  There is still no everyday unit of measure held against an item generally - nobody is going to keep a hundred and twenty conversion factors up to date - but an item the food bank buys and shelves by the pack rather than counting one by one may optionally record how many make up one pack and what to call one; most items have neither, and an item with no pack size recorded is exactly as it was before this existed.  The low stock threshold is optional, item by item, so an item nobody wants watched is simply left without one rather than defaulting to a warning nobody set.  Where a threshold is set, the system counts how many items have fallen below it, so an administrator can see at a glance whether anything needs reordering without reading down a hundred and twenty rows.

The description says what the item actually is, in the food bank's own words, where the name on its own does not: that half a kilo of pasta counts as one unit when rice is given as well, or what size of tin is meant.  It is shown on the maintenance screen and printed on the picking sheet beside the item name, so that the caveat reaches the person packing the bag rather than living in somebody's head.

The category groups the items - tinned goods, dry goods, toiletries and so on.  It is typed rather than chosen from a list: there are few enough categories that maintaining a separate list of them would be more trouble than it saves.  Every item has one and an item cannot be left without one, because the category is what two of the screens are ordered by and an item without one would have nowhere to sit on either of them.

Its capitalisation is settled by the system, so that an item typed as "tinned goods" and one typed as "Tinned Goods" end up in the same group.  This is worth its one oddity: an item typed as "UHT milk" is stored as "Uht Milk", because a system that left a word in capitals alone would let "UHT milk" and "uht milk" be two groups again.  The food bank would rather read "Uht Milk" than see the same category twice.  Nothing else about a category is corrected: two spellings that differ by more than their capitalisation are two categories, and noticing that is the administrator's job.

The stock item maintenance screen shows the description and the category, and lists the items by category, and by item name within each category.  So does the screen on which a picking list is amended.  The shelf number is the order for the two places where somebody is walking the warehouse and nowhere else: the printed picking sheet and the stock take.

Ordering by shelf number is a plain sort of the shelf label exactly as it is typed.  The system does not try to be clever about numbers inside it, so a shelf written "A10" sorts before "A2"; numbering the shelves so that the walk comes out in the right order is for whoever labels them, not something the system arranges.  A shelf number is not checked or corrected in any way - whatever is typed is what is stored and what is sorted on.
The stock take is run by a team leader - and, as with everything a team leader does, an administrator can do it too.  The team leader need not do the counting themselves.  The people walking the shelves with a clipboard are often volunteers who have no account and are never going to be given one, and a team leader can hand them a volunteer code to count with: one code, generated fresh, read out to whoever is counting that morning.  A volunteer signs on with that code alone, and it lets them onto the stock take - the shelf list of what to count with its groupings and crates, entering a count, saving a page - and onto nothing else at all: not the item maintenance list, not a pick list, not a referral, not a hand correction, not anything with a household's name on it.  The code lasts 14 days from the moment it was made and then stops working, and nothing is kept once it has lapsed.  When a code is generated it is shown once, together with the date and time it will stop working, so whoever hands it out knows how long it is good for.  Making another code leaves every earlier code valid until its own expiry time.  While a code is still working, an administrator sees a warning once it has less than 5 days left before it expires; the warning states its expiry time but cannot retrieve the code itself.  Once every code has lapsed the screen shows there is none, the same as before the first was made.  Whatever is counted on a code is recorded against the team leader who gave it out.  Amending the item list itself is administrator only.

A hand correction is not part of what a volunteer code reaches - it stays with the team leader, who is the person who would know a shelf had drifted from what the system believed.

Every stock item also belongs to a stock-take grouping - a further, coarser heading above the category, such as "Non-perishable", that the grouped stock take is organised by.  There are few enough groupings that they are administrator-maintained rather than typed, unlike the category.  Every item starts in the one grouping the food bank already has when this began, and an item newly added is put there too unless somebody chooses otherwise for it.

Some items are not counted on the shelf one at a time at all, but as a crate: several stock items shelved together behind one shelf number, in proportions the food bank already knows because that is how the supplier delivers them.  A crate belongs to one stock-take grouping, has its own unique shelf number, and names at least two of the stock items it contains.  For each of those items the food bank records two shares out of a hundred: how much of a counted crate is that item, and how much of a shortfall bought to make up that crate should be that item when shopping.  The two need not agree - a crate might arrive four parts tinned tomatoes to one part tinned beans, while a shortfall is made up differently - and each is complete on its own: the shares making up a counted crate add up to exactly a hundred, and so, separately, do the shares making up a shopping crate.  A crate's own shelf number puts it in the same shelf-walking order as everything else on the stock take: a crate sits among the individually-shelved items at the point its shelf number falls, not in a list of its own.

Counting a crate on the stock take is one line, not one line per item: a team leader enters how many crates are on the shelf, to one decimal place so a shelf with half a crate left need not be rounded to a whole number, and the food bank's own shares divide that figure among the items the crate contains, each item's count worked out and rounded on its own rather than adjusted to make a total agree.  An item that is a member of a crate is counted through the crate and does not also carry its own grouping - it is one or the other.  Should the two ever disagree with each other, or with what is actually on the shelves - a crate's shelf number no longer matching where its members are kept, an item belonging to more than one crate, or an item that is in neither a grouping nor a crate and so would never appear on any stock take - the food bank wants to be told about it, as something for an administrator to look at and put right, not as something that stops a stock take or a change to the item list being saved.  All of this is only ever about items the food bank is currently stocking: a retired item is on no stock take, so it is held to none of it - it does not make a shelf it happens to share need a crate, and it is not chased for a grouping it no longer needs.  Retiring an item that was a member of a crate leaves the crate alone: the crate keeps that member on its books and stays a valid crate, so that retiring an item is never a back door to breaking a crate, and the record of what a crate contained is not quietly rewritten every time the food bank stops stocking one of its items.

Some directly grouped items are counted in a convenient packing unit as well as one at a time: for example, Baby formula - Stage 1 may be put in Non-perishable and counted as individual tins or as boxes of a stated number of tins.  The packing unit says only how the counter may enter that one item's count; it never makes an item a crate or changes its stock-take grouping.  A team leader may enter a packing-unit count to one decimal place, and the system converts it to the nearest whole individual item before saving it, making clear what figure will be recorded.  A crate remains only for a mixed shelf of at least two named member items.

The weekly stock take is the single source of truth for how much stock the food bank has.  A count supersedes everything the system previously believed about that item: whatever was recorded before it is discarded, and the counted figure becomes the item's opening balance for the week that follows.  The food bank does not want to keep, or to be asked about, any history from before the previous stock take.

The difference between the count and the figure the system had been holding - the variance - is not recorded and is not reported on.  The food bank has no use for it: the count is the truth from that moment, and what the system believed a moment earlier is of no further interest.  Nothing needs to be able to tell a count that came out short from one that came out long, or either from any other way the figure has moved.

The food bank does not track shopping, donations or wastage: the count on the shelf next week says what the stock actually is, whatever happened to it.  But a team leader can correct an item's level by hand between one take and the next, when they discover it is wrong - the everyday reasons a shelf drifts from what the system believes, that the food bank does not need a name for.  The correction is entered as the amount the level is out by, up or down, and not as a fresh total to replace the old one - the same reasoning that makes an amount safer to enter than a total is why a stock take itself asks for a count and lets the system work out what changed, while a correction is the other way round and asks for the change directly.  Nothing is kept about why it happened or who made it, in the same way the variance between a stock take and what the system believed is not recorded: once the level is put right, the food bank has no further use for how it came to be wrong.  Making a correction is close to the stock take and belongs with the team leader who runs it - and, as with everything a team leader does, an administrator can do it too.  It is not, though, something a volunteer code reaches: a code is for counting the shelves, and a correction is the team leader's own judgement that the count and the shelf have since parted company.

So stock moves in three ways now - a stock take setting an opening balance, a parcel given to a household, and a hand correction - and between one take and the next, a parcel going out and a correction either way are the only things that change the level.

The count is entered a page at a time, forty items to a page, and each page is saved on its own.  A volunteer counting a hundred and twenty items should not lose the morning's work to one mistake, and should be able to stop halfway and carry on later.

Only the items whose number the volunteer has changed are saved.  An item left alone is not recorded at all, which means it either was counted and found correct or was never counted, and the food bank is content not to be able to tell those apart - it is not worth a volunteer ticking a box a hundred and twenty times to find out.

A stock take is not a thing the system keeps.  There is no record that one started, no record of which pages were done, and nothing to say a count was ever finished.  Nothing else changes the stock while a count is happening; the food bank arranges that between themselves rather than the system enforcing it.

#Picking list
Against a session there will be an option to view picking list.  If that picking list has not been created yet, then it will be done at this point.  For each referral, the two derived counts of adults and children the client sent will result via a series of rules into a model picking list.
This picking list can be easily modified (add new items, change quantities).  This generates a printable picking list page per referral, with a referral number and the stock items they require and quantities.

When the picking list is being amended, every stock item is shown, not only the ones already in the parcel, in the same order as the maintenance screen: by category, and by item name within the category.  An item the household is not getting shows a blank rather than a nought, so that the eye goes to the quantities that are there.  Adding something to a parcel is then a matter of typing a number against a line that is already on the screen.  The printed sheet is the other way round: it lists only what the household is actually getting, in shelf order, so that the person picking walks the warehouse once.

The screen on which the picking list is maintained shows the household's preferences beside the parcel, because whoever is deciding what goes in the bag is the only person who can act on them - a parcel that cannot be eaten is no use to anybody.  The preferences are those questions on the referral form that are marked as preferences; the rest of the answers are not shown, because they are not about what goes in the parcel.  Which questions those are is decided by the form, so the server hands over all the answers and the screen picks out the ones it asked as preferences.

The screen is split down the middle: the parcel on one side, the preferences on the other, and each side pages on its own, because a long parcel and a long list of preferences do not run out at the same time.

Almost all of that reading-and-adding is done for the team leader rather than by them, through a combination of rules and the model parcels.  The counts the client sends already produce a model picking list, and the food bank keeps a further small set of preference rules saying which stock item a preference answer means and how much of it a household of a given size gets; those rules too are applied when the picking list is created, so a team leader opens a parcel that already has the household's toiletries and household goods in it.  The intention is that the team leader has almost nothing to do - the exception is a dietary requirement or other free-form answer the rules cannot turn into a quantity, described below.  The rules belong to the referral application along with the questions they read, for the same reason the questions do: they change when the form changes, and a rule that outlived the question it asks about would be worse than no rule.  The server is not given the rules and does not read them.  It is given the stock items they came out to, and it still decides on its own which households get a parcel at all and which model parcel each one starts from.

A preference asks for at least its quantity.  Where the model parcel already contains the item, the household gets the larger of the two and not the sum of them - a rule saying two of something means the household should end up with two, not with two on top of whatever the model parcel happened to hold.

The rules only reach a parcel as it is created.  Once a household has a parcel, it is the team leader's, and nothing the referral application sends afterwards changes it; a picking list that reshuffled itself under somebody who was halfway through editing it would be worse than one that never helped at all.

Where the rules cannot turn a preference into a quantity - a free-text answer, an answer with no rule, a rule needing something the referral does not ask, or a name that is not exactly one stock item - the item is put on the parcel as needing attention rather than guessed at or dropped.  It sits with the other lines, naming the item and saying plainly that somebody has to decide, and the team leader either puts a number against it or takes it off.  Until every one of them is settled the parcel cannot be reviewed, and so it cannot be printed and the household cannot be marked as having received it.  That is deliberate: a request the food bank could not work out is a question for a person, and the parcel is where they are already looking.  It is the item that needs attention and not the answer, because what a team leader has to decide is how much of a particular thing goes in the bag.

Reviewing a parcel is not undone by changing it afterwards.  The same team leader who reviews a parcel is the one who goes on to correct it - swapping one tin for another as stock runs out, say - and being made to review it again after every such change would be a step that caught nobody out and slowed everybody down.  A parcel stays reviewed, and printable, however many times its contents are corrected after the fact.

Beside the parcel's contents there is its pick-list information: the things whoever is packing the bag has to be told about this household, in words rather than quantities - the allergies, what nobody in the house can eat, the preference somebody wrote out in their own hand.  It is put together when the picking list is created, from those answers on the referral form that are marked as belonging on it.  Which answers those are is decided by the form, in the same way the preference rules are, so the server is handed the finished words rather than the questions and never reads an answer to work them out.

Like the preference rules, it reaches a parcel only as that parcel is created.  From then on the writing is the team leader's, the same as the lines are: they can change it, add to it, or clear it away entirely, right up until the session is confirmed.  Nothing the referral application sends afterwards overwrites it.  Opening a picking list again to catch a household who arrived late must not quietly undo a correction somebody made that morning, and a note about an allergy is exactly the correction that must not go missing.

What is printed is what was saved.  The sheet carries the pick-list information as it stands on the parcel and not the answers as they read today, because the person packing the bag is working from the paper in their hand and it has to say what the team leader meant it to say.  The limit is twelve hundred characters, which is room for the two longest answers the form allows together with the labels saying which is which.

On the printed sheet there is the picking number and the household's name, and each item on it carries its description under the item name.  Every sheet has the name on it, not only the deliveries - the person carrying the bag has to hand it to somebody, and a number does not do that.  If it is a delivery the sheet also says DELIVERY and carries the address, the postcode and the phone number, so the driver can find the door and ring the bell.  The reason for referral is never on the picking sheet, which gets carried round a hall and left on tables.  It appears on one document only, and that is the listener sheet.

Once every parcel on a session's picking list has been reviewed, the food bank can ask what that session is going to take off the shelves.  The answer is a line for each stock item the session's parcels between them call for, giving the quantity the session needs and the quantity currently in stock, so that a team leader can see in one place where there is not going to be enough and do something about it before the morning.  Only the items the session actually needs are listed.  The whole item list with a hundred blank lines in it is the stock take screen, and this is not that screen: an item nobody on that session is getting is not something anybody wants to read past.

A household whose referral has been cancelled is left out of the total, on the same grounds as their sheet is not printed - nothing is picked for a household that is not coming.  Their parcel is not waited for either: holding the whole session's figure until somebody reviews a parcel that will never be picked would be the same mistake as holding up the printing for one.  A household already marked as having attended is still counted, even though their parcel has already come off the shelves, because the figure is what the session as a whole asks for and not what is left to pick; the question is asked before the session starts, when the two are the same thing.  A household marked as not having turned up is counted for the same reason - their parcel was still picked and packed, and the only household the session does not have to find food for is one that was cancelled before the morning.  It waits for the reviews and not for the list to be confirmed, which is the same point printing waits for and for the same reason: a reviewed parcel is one somebody has settled, and until a parcel is reviewed it may still carry a line saying somebody has to decide.  There is no honest total to be had while that line is there, and a figure that quietly left it out would be worse than no figure at all.  Waiting for confirmation instead would give the answer after the work it is meant to help with, which is no use to anybody.

Once the session itself is confirmed, the comparison is no longer offered.  By then every attended household's stock has already come off the shelves while their parcel still counts towards what was needed, so the figure would be comparing a session that is finished against a shelf it can no longer do anything about - a shortage the food bank could not act on even if it saw one.

#SMS reminders and replies
The team leader running a session can send the households on it a reminder by text message.  It is a button on the screen the session is run from and nothing sends a reminder on its own: whether the session is going ahead, and whether the list of households is settled enough to text them, are things the person running it knows and a timer does not.

Every household on the session is sent one, except those whose referral has been cancelled or rejected - they are not coming, and a reminder telling them to is worse than no reminder at all.  A household still waiting to be reviewed is sent one like anybody else: they hold a place on the session and they may well turn up, so they are told when to come.  The picking list follows the same rule, so the people running a session have a parcel, referral and name for every household they text.

A household that has already been sent a reminder for that session is not sent another however many times the button is pressed, so a second press reaches only the households referred since the first.  The referral records that a reminder was sent to it, and whether one has gone is shown against each household on the session.

Households can reply to the reminder, and the replies come back into the system rather than to somebody's phone.  A reply is matched to a household by the number it was sent from, and recorded against the referral for the soonest session still to come.  A household referred to two sessions has replied about the nearer one, near enough always, and a session that has already happened is not a candidate at all - so a reply that arrives the morning after is a loose reply rather than a note filed against a session nobody will look at again.  A session stops being a candidate the day after it runs, or the moment it is confirmed or cancelled, whichever comes first - not the moment its start time passes, because a delivery can still be on the road well after the time it was due to start, and a household can just as well text back while their own session is under way.

A reply from a number with no open referral is not thrown away.  It is kept as a loose reply, on a screen of its own that only administrators see.  Somebody has still texted the food bank and somebody should read it, and a system that silently swallowed those would be worse than one that never took replies at all.

When a reminder cannot be sent, that is recorded against the household in the same place as a reply, so the person running the session sees it where they are already looking rather than in a log they would have to know to open.  A failure is recorded as already read: it needs to be visible, but it is not somebody waiting for an answer.

A household the food bank cannot text at all is treated exactly the same way.  There may be no number on the referral, or the number there may be a perfectly good one that is not a mobile - and the food bank often cannot tell which until it tries.  Either way nothing was delivered, so it is recorded against the household as a failure like any other rather than passed over in silence.  A household nobody can reach is a household somebody may need to ring, and that is only actionable if it is on the screen.

A household whose reminder failed has not been reminded, and the system does not pretend otherwise: pressing the button again tries them again.  A failure is usually the network or the provider having a bad minute, and the food bank would rather text a household twice than let one go without because of it.  A number that is simply wrong will fail on every press, and that is the food bank being told, once per attempt, that the number they hold is no good.

A household collecting and a household having their parcel delivered are told different things, and both greet the household by their first name and say who is texting.  A collection reminder reads `Hi FIRST NAME this is Guildford Food Bank. Please collect your parcel on DATE at TIME from LOCATION. Any problems let us know.`  A delivery reminder reads `Hi FIRST NAME this is Guildford Food Bank. Your parcel will be delivered on DATE, WINDOW. Someone must be home to receive it. Any problems let us know.`, where WINDOW is the delivery window and never a single moment - the household knows where they live, and their address is the one thing a delivery reminder must not carry, but a van round still cannot promise a doorstep at a precise time, only that it will be there between two times.

Staff can text a household back from that same screen, so a household that asks a question gets an answer rather than a reminder that ignores them.  What staff send is held with the rest of the conversation, and it is bound by the same rule as the reminder: no name, and nothing that says who the number belongs to.

The team leader running the session reads the messages and answers them, and so does an administrator.  What a household texts can be as personal as the reason they were referred, which team leaders are otherwise not shown, and that is accepted here for the same reason the listener sheet is: the person on the day is the one who can act on it.  A household who texts to say they are running late, or cannot lift the bag, or are not coming after all, needs the person in the hall to know - not somebody who will read it on Monday.  The loose replies, from numbers with no session behind them, stay with administrators.

Every message is either read or unread, but a reminder, a staff reply and a failure are always recorded already read the moment they are written - none of the three is somebody waiting for an answer.  Only a household's own reply is ever unread.  The screen the session is run from shows the total number of unread messages prominently, and keeps it up to date as they arrive if it can.  Against each household there is a button showing how many messages there are and how many are unread, marked out while any are unread, and pressing it opens that household's messages within the list rather than on another screen.  That count keeps itself up to date as well if it can.

Opening a household's messages marks them read, and there is nothing further to press.  The count is there to say that nobody has looked at this household yet, and once somebody has looked that is no longer true.  Anyone who wants a message kept in front of them writes it down somewhere it will be acted on, which is what they would do anyway.

The counts are of what has come in - replies from the household, and the failures - and not of what the food bank has sent.  A count that went up when staff answered a message would be counting their own work back at them.  What is shown when the messages are opened is the whole conversation in order, both sides of it, because a reply on its own does not say what it was replying to.

Messages are kept for thirty days and then deleted - the reminders, the replies, the failures and the loose replies alike.  A text message is a conversation about one session and it stops being useful within days of that session; keeping a household's own words for longer than the food bank has any use for them is holding personal data for the sake of it.  This happens on its own, nightly, and needs nobody to remember it.

The text messages are sent and received through TheSMSWorks, and the only thing the food bank gives them is the number to text.  A reminder or a referrer message greets its recipient by their first name and says it is from Guildford Food Bank, but carries nothing beyond that first name to identify anyone - never a surname, and never an address.

What a household texts back is theirs, and it is held with the rest of their details and forgotten with them.

Administrators can see every text message the food bank is still holding, not only the loose replies, on a screen of their own.  What is listed there by default is one row per phone number that has something on it besides a reminder having gone out - a reply, a failure, or something staff sent - because a number that was reminded and never heard from again has nothing on it for anybody to do, and a food bank texting a session's worth of households would otherwise bury the numbers that need attention under the ones that do not.  Opening a number that is listed shows everything within the thirty days already described for that number, the reminders included, because a reply answers a reminder and the two belong on the screen together.

What an administrator is actually told needs doing is smaller than what they can see.  A household's reply that nobody has read yet needs an administrator when it is a loose reply, or when the session it belonged to has since been confirmed or cancelled - the team leader who was running that session is not going to open it again, and nobody else was going to either unless it is put in front of somebody.  A reply still sitting on a session that has not yet happened, or is under way, stays the team leader's to read; an administrator can look at it from this screen, but it does not count towards what administrators are told needs doing, because reading it is somebody else's job until that session is over.

A message remembers the session it actually arrived about.  Moving a household to a later session afterwards, as administrators can, must not quietly move an old message with it - a text that came in while a household was booked onto Tuesday's session was about Tuesday's session, whatever session that household sits on by the time anybody reads it.

Reading one message from the administrators' screen marks only that message read.  It does not clear anything else - not another loose reply, not a session's own unread count, and not the messages a team leader is still working through.  None of this creates a record of who is dealing with a message or that anybody has taken it on; a message is still simply read or unread, the same as everywhere else in this feature.

When a referral's collection method is `referrer_collect`, every parcel-related text message about it - the reminder and anything staff send about it - goes to the referrer's own phone number instead of the referee's, because it is the referrer coming to collect, not the household.  The reminder reads `Hi FIRST NAME this is Guildford Food Bank. Please collect your client's parcel as arranged on DATE at TIME from LOCATION. Any problems let us know.`, using the referrer's own first name and never the household's, and it does not otherwise reuse the household's own collection or delivery wording.  This wording does not yet say which parcel it is about when the same referrer is currently collecting for more than one household at once - that case is still open.  See `OPEN-QUESTIONS.md`.

A referrer's own reply is not a household's reply and is never treated as one.  A reply from a number that is currently a referrer collecting one or more open `referrer_collect` parcels is kept as a referrer message, distinct from both a household's own reply and a loose one, and only administrators see it.  It is not filed against one household automatically - a referrer may be collecting for several households and on several days, and guessing which one would be exactly the wrong kind of guess - but administrators are shown enough about each of that referrer's currently open `referrer_collect` referrals to make the judgement themselves, without being shown the reason for referral or anything else about the household beyond what identifies which one it might be.  Team leaders never see a referrer message, the households it might belong to, or that it exists at all - it carries none of what running a session needs, and it may well belong to a household on a session they are not running at all.

#Listener sheet
The food bank gives selected volunteers - listeners - the job of talking to households at a session, and the listener sheet is what they are given to do it from.

There is one listener sheet for a session, not one per household, and it is printed.  It carries, for each household: the pick number, the name, other information, the reason for crisis, the secondary reason, whether the household needs help with fuel, whether this is the household's first time, and the Christmas voucher instruction.  Nothing else - not the address, not the phone number, not what is in the parcel.  The pick number is on it because the listener sheet and the picking sheets are carried round the same hall, and a listener and a picker have to be able to match a household between the two.  Every household on the sheet has one - there is no household on it without a number to give.

The first-time marker and the voucher instruction are the same two the team leader is shown on the Run a session screen, worked out the same way and described under "Christmas voucher and first-time selection" below: `First time` or `Admin` or neither for whether the household is new, and - only while the session falls in the Christmas voucher period - one of `Provide voucher for this client`, `Client has already received voucher` or `Refer to administrators for voucher`.  The listener sees them because the listener is the one actually talking to the household: a new household to welcome, a voucher to hand over.  They are worked out afresh each time the sheet is produced, so an administrator's later first-time decision shows straight away, and they say only whether the household is new and what to do about a voucher - never the date of a previous session, never anything more about the referral.

The sheet lists the households who are coming to the session in person.  A household whose referral was cancelled or rejected is not on it: they are not coming, and a listener sheet is a list of named people and what went wrong for each of them, so somebody the food bank turned away has no business on it.  Neither is a delivery, for a different reason: nobody walks in for a delivery, so a listener will never have that conversation, and printing the household's name and crisis on a sheet carried round the hall would be exposing somebody who was never going to be there.  A household still waiting to be reviewed is on the sheet, because whether an administrator has got round to reading the referral says nothing about whether the household is turning up.

Producing the listener sheet is a team leader job, like everything else that happens on the day, and it is the only place a team leader sees the reason for referral.  The link sits on the selected Run a session screen alongside Print all pick lists.  The sheet carries a pick number for every household on it.  If a household has been referred to the session since the pick list was made and has not yet been picked for, it has no pick number to print, and the food bank would rather refuse the whole sheet than hand a listener one with gaps in it or numbers that do not match the picking sheets.  So producing the listener sheet is refused outright until every household coming to the session in person has been picked for - the same household arriving late that already calls for a picking list to be opened again, described under "Picking list" above.  Everywhere else - the picking list, the printed picking sheet, the referral as a team leader reads it - the reason stays with the administrators.

The listener sheet is the most sensitive thing the food bank prints: a list of named households and what went wrong for each of them.  It is meant for the volunteers chosen to do the listening and for nobody else.

What becomes of the sheet after the session is handled by the food bank as a matter of process, not by the system.  Looking after the paper is something the volunteers are asked to do properly; the system does nothing towards it, and does not keep a record of a sheet having been produced or of who produced it.

After picking is complete, modifications can be made to the list.

#Fuel help list
Households say on the referral form whether they need help with gas or electricity.  It is a fixed question rather than an ordinary one because the food bank reports on it, and because somebody follows it up: the fuel help list is what that person works from.

The list is a screen rather than a printed sheet, and it is laid out so that it can be copied straight into a spreadsheet.  The people who deal with fuel work in Excel, and the food bank would rather they pasted a list than retyped one and got a phone number wrong.

A household is on the list when four things are true: they said they needed help with fuel; they were given their parcel, whether they collected it or it was delivered to them; the session has been confirmed; and that session was within the last two weeks.  The two weeks are counted as a fortnight of dates ending today, so a session a fortnight ago yesterday has dropped off.  The list is ordered by the date of the session, oldest first, so that whoever is working through it deals with the households closest to dropping off the end.

A line on the list is a referral rather than a household.  A household given a parcel twice inside a fortnight would appear twice, and nothing tries to fold those together - being fed twice in two weeks is not what is meant to happen, and a list that quietly hid it would be hiding the thing worth noticing.

Each of those four earns its place.  There is nothing to follow up for a household that was expected and never got their parcel, so it is attendance and not a booked place that puts somebody on the list - and a delivery counts as having attended, here as everywhere else.  The session has to be confirmed because until it is signed off its outcomes can still be taken back, and a household whose attendance is about to be reversed should not have been rung about their gas bill in the meantime.  The two weeks make it a rolling list: the point is what has happened since it was last looked at, and a fuel crisis from a year ago is not a reason to ring somebody today.

Each row carries the household's name, date of birth, address, postcode and phone number, whether they need help with fuel, the date of the session, and the answers given on the referral form.  The name and address are who is being helped and where they live, and the phone number is there because the follow-up is a phone call.  The date of birth is on the row too: it identifies the household to whoever is following the fuel bill up, and the food bank wants it there.  The fuel flag is carried as a plain yes or no rather than left for the reader to assume from a household's presence on the list - it is what the client form is showing everywhere else it shows this question, so the fuel help list shows it the same way rather than making it a special case.

The two questions that follow the fuel question - whether the household is on a pre-payment meter, and whether they will let us ring them about it - are ordinary questions on the form rather than fixed columns.  So the list carries the form answers as they were given and the screen picks those two out to show, in the same way the listener sheet shows "Cause Details".  The system does not leave anybody off the list on the strength of them: every household meeting the four conditions is listed, and the person about to make the call reads the answers and decides whether to make it.  That judgement belongs to a person, and a household who said no to a call may still be somebody worth writing to.

The list does not carry the reason for referral.  A fuel administrator is being told that a household needs help with a fuel bill, which is all they need to know to help with it; why the household was referred is not theirs, and the listener sheet remains the only place outside the administrators where the reason appears.

It does not carry the household numbers, or whether the parcel was delivered, either.  Neither of them bears on helping with a fuel bill.

#Sending referrals to the spreadsheet
The food bank keeps its records in a Google spreadsheet and intends to carry on doing so.  The spreadsheet this system writes to is a different one, built for the extract and holding only the anonymised record described under "Forgetting a referral" above - the food bank's own spreadsheet, with everything else it holds, is untouched by this system and stays exactly the record-keeping tool it already was.  This system feeds the extract sheet rather than replacing anything, so every referral ends up on it.  Nobody is going to copy, paste or import anything: a file to download was offered and turned down on exactly that ground, so the system writes into the spreadsheet itself.

An administrator starts the extract.  It is an administrator's action and nobody else's: before it starts, the system says that it might take some time and asks whether to continue.  Only after the administrator agrees does the browser ask separately for permission to write to Google Sheets.  That permission is for the administrator's Google account, not their food bank sign-on, and the Google access token is kept only in the browser while the extract is running.  Neither the token nor any other Google credential is sent to or stored by the food bank system.

The spreadsheet itself is fixed for each deployment.  Its id and the public Google OAuth client id are deployment configuration, including separate values for test.  The server exposes those values only to an administrator starting an extract.  Google access is made directly from the browser, so the server has no service account and never calls Google.

What is sent is a session that has been signed off, and every referral on it - the cancelled and the rejected ones included, because a referral that was turned away still happened and the spreadsheet is the record of what happened.  Nothing is sent before its session is signed off.  Once a session has been signed off nothing on it can change any more, so what is sent is final: the spreadsheet is only ever added to, and nothing already on it is ever rewritten.  Alongside its referral rows, a session's extract also carries one stock-usage summary, described below; the two travel together under the one reservation and the one mark-as-extracted, not through any queue or reservation of the summary's own.

The browser takes one unextracted, signed-off session at a time, oldest first.  The server reserves that session before returning its referrals, so two administrators cannot extract the same session at once.  The browser writes every referral on the reserved session, then tells the server to mark it extracted.  A session goes across whole or not at all.  Half a session's households arriving one week and the rest the next would leave somebody reading the spreadsheet unable to tell a quiet week from an unfinished one.  Once a session has been marked extracted it is not selected again.

After twenty sessions the browser stops and asks the administrator whether to continue with another batch.  It shows progress while it runs.  Closing or reloading the browser, or the machine sleeping, stops the extract; a later administrator starts another batch.  A reservation that is not marked extracted expires so that an interrupted extract cannot leave a session unavailable forever.  The server records enough information to reject a completion request from a different or expired reservation.  A reservation is given back only by expiring: a browser whose write to the spreadsheet fails simply stops, and the session it was holding waits out the reservation before anybody can pick it up again.  Nothing is lost by that and nothing is sent twice, but it does mean a failed extract cannot be retried on the spot.

A batch running out of sessions to hand out is not the same thing as the work being finished, and the administrator is never told the one when the other is true.  When a write has failed, the sessions still reserved are unavailable until they expire, so the next batch is handed nothing while those sessions are still plainly outstanding.  What the screen shows at that point is how many sessions are still to go, not that the extract is done - a batch that ends with sessions still waiting has to say so, and say that they are reserved and will come back shortly.  Telling an administrator the spreadsheet is up to date when it is not is the one thing this must not do, because they would have no reason to look again.

The sheet has a hidden metadata sheet holding the mapping from each dynamic answer key to its column.  The browser reads that mapping before its first write, puts all answers for one key in that key's column, and writes the metadata sheet whenever it adds a key.  This mapping is spreadsheet state, not food bank database state.

The row carries only the household's postcode and date of birth as personal information.  It does not carry any client or referrer name, address, email address or telephone number.  It also carries the reason they were referred, the answers they gave on the form, and the administrator's comment if the referral was reviewed.

Alongside those household rows, the session's extract also carries a stock-usage summary: one line per stock item the session actually issued to a household, naming the item and giving the whole number issued for it, summed across every parcel on the session.  This is what the session's parcels currently show as issued - the ledger keeps no history of an earlier attempt once an outcome has been taken back and redone, so there is nothing else to sum, and nothing about a stock movement itself, including its identifier or whether it has individually been through an extract before, is part of what leaves the server this way.  An item retired since is still listed if it was issued for this session, the same way a referral reason retired since keeps its place on a referral already made against it.  An item the session never issued, or whose issued quantity comes to zero, is simply left off the summary; a session that issued nothing still has a summary, an empty one rather than a missing one.

The row says which session the household was on by its date and the place it was held, not by any internal identifier.  The spreadsheet is read by people, and the reason the referred-to reason appears as its wording rather than its code applies here for the same reason: somebody looking down a column wants to see where a household went.

Who may see the spreadsheet is nothing to do with this system: everyone it is shared with sees every column on it, and none of the care taken over who sees what inside the system reaches it.  An administrator's comment can name a referrer or set down a suspicion about one, which inside the system is shown to administrators only and on the spreadsheet is shown to whoever has it open.

Each row carries the referral's own identifier.  Sending is the one part of this system that reaches outside it, and the spreadsheet cannot be part of the same all-or-nothing step as the food bank's own records - so if the sending succeeds and the marking-as-sent then fails, a session is sent twice.  That is the way round it should fail: a row that arrives twice can be found and deleted, and a household that quietly never arrived cannot be found at all.  The identifier on the row is what makes the first of those possible.

The browser never retries a Google Sheets write automatically after a timeout: it may have succeeded.  The administrator can inspect the referral identifiers in the sheet, remove a duplicate if necessary, and start another extract later.

#Model parcels and the household grid
The model picking lists and the household grid belong to the administrator, to look at as well as to change.  A team leader never needs to see either of them: the picking sheet in their hand already says what the household is getting, and the model it was copied from a week ago tells a picker nothing they can do anything about.  So neither appears on a team leader's screens at all.

The "series of rules" that turns a household into a model picking list is a lookup, not a calculation.

There is a table of model picking lists.  Each has a name as its key and its contents as JSON - the stock items and quantities that make up that parcel.

The name of a model picking list cannot be changed once it exists, because the grid refers to it by that name.  A model that is wrongly named is deleted and set up again, which forces somebody to go back to the grid and say what each household size should now get - which is the point, since a rename that silently left the grid pointing at the old name would be the same mistake made quietly.

Separately there is a grid of every possible operational household size.  Adults run from 1 to 5 and children from 0 to 5, so there are 30 entries.  Both are the counts the client derived and sent, whose ages the server does not know.  A household larger than 5 operational adults counts as 5, and more than 5 operational children counts as 5.  Each grid entry holds just the name of the model picking list to use for that household size, so several household sizes can share one model parcel and changing that parcel updates all of them.  The grid is maintained as a whole and updated in one go, so it can be stored as a single row.

A referral must have at least one derived adult, so every referral maps to a real grid entry.  The household grid must provide a model parcel for every household size holding a place before a session pick list can be generated.  Generation is all-or-nothing: if a required grid entry is missing, no parcels are created until an administrator completes the grid.

The model picking lists are not versioned.  When a picking list is created for a session, the contents of the relevant model are copied onto each referral's parcel.  From then on that parcel holds its own contents and is unaffected by any later change to the model or the grid.  The parcel does not record which model it came from - only what is in it.  Opening a picking list again, before its session is confirmed, creates parcels for any referral holding a place which has arrived or been moved into the session since it was first opened, but never changes an existing parcel.  This makes late households visible without silently overwriting a picker's work.  Once the session is confirmed, no further parcels are added.

#Christmas voucher and first-time selection
An administrator maintains one Christmas-voucher date range, with a start date and an end date, on the administrator's Master Data screen.  The voucher scheme is active for a session whose date falls within that range, including its first and last day.

First-time checking is a separate, dedicated screen in the administrator's referral-review process.  It is the step that records, for every referral, the date of that client's last session or the explicit value that the client has no previous referral; the voucher-date screen does not make that decision.  The screen has a Save action: saving either choice sets the referral's first-time-review value from `unreviewed` to the chosen last-session date or `no previous referral`.  Saving is not conditional on the referral's own status: a rejected or cancelled referral, or one on a confirmed session, can still have its first-time-review value set.  This will not come up in practice, since the screen is not offered against a referral in that state, but the save action itself does not need to check for it.  When this feature is introduced, every existing referral is backfilled as `no previous referral`.  The first-time chooser shows every session, not only ones whose households attended.  A referral recorded as No Show or Not in remains visible but cannot be selected.  A referral still pending is included and can be selected, whether it is awaiting approval of its referrer, an administrator's review, or simply the session itself.

For a pick list whose session falls in the voucher range, the Run a session screen shows the team leader exactly one voucher instruction for each household.  Until the administrator has made the previous-referral decision, it says `Refer to administrators for voucher`.  Once they have, it says `Client has already received voucher` where the stored last-session date is in the voucher range, and `Provide voucher for this client` where there is no previous referral or that date is outside the range.  This instruction is worked out afresh whenever the screen is read, not copied into the parcel when its pick list is generated: an administrator may make the decision after the pick list exists, and the screen must show the current decision.  It is not on the printed picking sheet, which is carried round the hall and left on tables; the voucher is handed over at the session, so the instruction belongs on the screen the team leader is working from.

The same previous-referral decision also tells the team leader on the Run a session screen whether a household is new.  A referral reviewed by an administrator and recorded as having no previous session is marked `First time`.  A referral for which the administrator has not yet made that decision is marked `Admin`.  A recorded previous-session date is not a first-time notification and carries neither mark.

The first-time marker and the voucher instruction also appear on the listener sheet, worked out the same way and afresh each time it is produced.  That is the only other place they are shown; see "Listener sheet" above for why the listener needs them.

#Target stock lists and shopping
The food bank keeps a handful of named target stock lists - a normal week, the run-up to Christmas, the school holidays - each one the quantities it wants on the shelves of the items it buys.  More sessions and more demand around Christmas mean a different, higher set of targets than an ordinary week, which is why there is more than one list rather than one set of numbers that everybody argues about twice a year.  An administrator maintains the lists: which items are on a list, and how many of each the food bank wants to hold.  A list need not name every stock item, only the ones it is buying for - a target of nought is not a way of saying that, since it means exactly the same thing as leaving the item off the list, and only one of those two ways of saying it exists.  A list's name can be changed after it is created, unlike a model picking list's - nothing else in the system refers to a target stock list by name, so renaming one does not leave anything else pointing at the old name.

There is no notion of a default or currently-active list.  A team leader chooses one explicitly every time they go shopping, and is not offered one already selected - so that they are looking at the list and deciding it is the right one for what is coming up, rather than printing whatever the system happens to have open.

A target stock list holds, for each item on it, that item's own key and name as they stood when the line was put on the list, not a live link back to the stock item table.  This is deliberate: a stock item can be renamed or retired independently of any list that mentions it, and a list is not required to stay in step with the catalogue in order to keep working.  Keeping the name is exactly what makes it possible to catch and show a discrepancy later - a name that no longer matches, or a key that no longer resolves, is only visible against something to compare it with.  Reconciling a list against the current catalogue - noticing a rename, or that an item on a list no longer exists or is no longer active - is something the administrator who maintains the lists deals with, not something that gets in the way of a team leader trying to do the shopping.

A line on the list can also be a whole crate rather than a single item, held to a number of crates rather than a whole number of items - the food bank might reasonably want half a crate more than it currently holds - and kept as the same kind of snapshot, by the crate's own name rather than a live link to it.  A stock item that is currently a member of a crate cannot itself be given a new individual target on a list being saved from scratch: the crate is what gets bought, item by item, once it is one.  This does not reach backwards - a target already on a list for an item before it became a crate member, or for a crate since deleted, is not taken away from under the administrator.  It stays on the list exactly as it was saved, shown as something needing their attention, until they remove it or replace it themselves.

A team leader, who does the food shopping, chooses one of the lists and is told what to buy: for each item on that list, the target quantity less what the last stock take says is currently held, and only for items where that figure is greater than zero.  The food bank does not otherwise keep a record of what was bought or when - a shopping list is worked out fresh, each time, from the list chosen and the stock position at that moment, and is not itself saved anywhere.

A team leader may also see a target stock list whole, target quantities and all, not only the shopping list worked out from one.  There is nothing on a target stock list a team leader does not already see for themselves: they are the one who counts what is on the shelves each week and the one standing in the stock room, so a target figure for an item that happens to be well stocked already tells them nothing a walk around the shelves would not.

#Fresh-food shopping list
The fresh-food shopping list is a separate calculation from the standing target-stock shopping list.  A team leader chooses a session date range, beginning today and ending on the end of the current week unless they select another end date.  For every session in that range, the browser asks for each active referral which does not already have a pick list to have its pick list created.  The server cannot create these lists as one bulk operation: the browser applies the maintained preference rules to each referral and creates the lists one at a time.  Existing pick lists are not recreated or changed.

Once that work has finished, the browser obtains the stock requirement across every pick list whose session is not yet complete.  A `-1` quantity is ignored rather than treated as a requirement.  For every other item, the shopping quantity is the requirement less the stock level, with the stock level treated as zero where the last stock take is negative; only a positive result is put on the list.  The list is worked out afresh and is not saved.

Whether an item may use the stock already held, or must be bought fresh regardless of that stock, is deliberately not decided here.  It must be settled before the fresh-food list can be built.

#Stock requirement report
Separately from the fresh-food shopping list, an administrator can ask for the running total of what every session still to come is going to need: for a chosen cut-off date, the quantity of each stock item called for by every parcel on a pick list whose session is not yet complete and whose session date falls on or before that date.  A session already confirmed has finished asking anything of the shelves and is left out, the same reasoning as everywhere else this comparison is made.

The total also has a floor: the start of the current week.  A session from further back that nobody has confirmed is not the ordinary case, and this report is about what is still being planned for, not a place old bookkeeping surfaces by accident - so a session dated before the current week does not add to the total however long it has sat unconfirmed.  This is not something the administrator chooses; the cut-off date is the only thing asked for, and the floor moves with the calendar on its own.

A `-1` line - one still needing a team leader's decision - is left out of the total rather than treated as a requirement, the same as it is for the fresh-food shopping list.  A cancelled parcel is left out too: nothing is picked for a household that is not coming.  Unlike the session-by-session comparison a team leader uses on the day, this total does not wait for every parcel to be reviewed - it looks across many sessions at once, most of them not yet picked, and a total that only appeared once picking was finished would arrive too late for the planning it is for.

The report gives the total required, not a comparison against what is on the shelf - it does not subtract stock on hand.  It only covers a referral once a pick list exists for its session; a session nobody has generated a pick list for yet has nothing to add to the total, the same limit the fresh-food shopping list works under.

This is an administrator's report.  A team leader does not see it: it is about planning ahead across sessions, not about the one session they are running.

#Session maintenance
After the session, each referee's pick list is reviewed before an attendance outcome can be recorded. Each referee is then marked as attended or did not show.  A delivery is marked the same way - delivered counts as attended and not in counts as did not show, because they are the same two outcomes under the names the round uses.  If they attended, the stock is updated.  Otherwise the parcel will be unpacked.

An outcome can be taken back while the session is still open.  Marking a household as not having shown up, after marking them as attended, puts the parcel's stock back: nothing left the building, so nothing should have come off the shelves.  There is no hand correction to put a mis-tap right with any more, so being able to say "no, they did not come after all" is the only way to fix one, and a team leader must not be made to live with a wrong figure until the next count.  Marking the same outcome again is not a change and is always accepted, because a team leader will tap twice and the stock must still only move once.

Confirming the session is the end of it.  Once a session is confirmed no outcome on it can be changed, because a session that has been signed off must not have its figures move underneath it afterwards.

The session is then closed.  Every referee on the session must be marked either attended (or delivered) or absent (or not in) before the session can be closed, so a session is never closed with people unaccounted for and stock figures that do not add up.  If anyone is still unmarked the close is refused and the ones outstanding are named.

#Platform usage monitoring
The system runs on Cloudflare's free plan, which caps how much a Worker and a D1 database may be used before Cloudflare starts refusing requests or queries.  So that the food bank can tell whether it is safely inside those caps, or needs to move to a paid plan, the system records its own usage against Cloudflare every day: how many requests the Worker served and how many failed, how much processing time and how many outgoing calls each request needed, and how many rows the database read and wrote and how much space it is using.  Processing time is a single top-end figure (the slowest invocations, not a typical one) rather than a full spread across the day, and a failed request is counted once regardless of why it failed — the report does not need to say why a request was slow or which limit it hit.

Processing time is shown against Cloudflare's own per-invocation limit for reference, but it does not by itself mark a day as worrying or count toward the alert below: the figure covers every kind of invocation the Worker handles, including its own nightly maintenance run, not only requests the food bank's own use produces, so a high reading here does not mean the food bank is pushing the system towards the cap.  A Worker error, by contrast, is worrying the moment there is even one — not a rate or a proportion of requests.

An administrator can review this for any date range: for each day, the actual figures alongside Cloudflare's published caps, with the days and measures running close to or over a cap marked so they stand out rather than needing to be read off one by one.  The same figures also feed an alert: a count of how many of the last fourteen days had any measure marked that way, so a problem developing against the free plan's caps is visible without opening the detailed report.

This is an administrator's report, about running the system rather than about the food bank's own work, so nobody else sees it.

How close to a cap counts as running close to it - and, for the average-subrequests figure, which Cloudflare puts no cap on at all - is not yet settled.  See OPEN-QUESTIONS.md.
